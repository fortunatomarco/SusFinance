Good morning. Our paper is The Determinants of ESG Ratings: Rater Ownership Matters, by Dragon Yongjun Tang, Jiali Yan and Yaqiong Yao, published in the Journal of Accounting Research this year.

The question behind it is deceptively simple. If a company happens to share a large shareholder with the agency that rates it — what the authors call a sister firm — does that company get a better ESG rating than it otherwise would? And if it does, is that because the shared owner is genuinely helping the rater do its job, or because the owner is leaning on the rater?

We have split this into four parts. I will set up the question, then my colleagues will take you through the data and method, what the data actually shows, and finally what it means for investors, managers and regulators.

Let me start with why this paper matters before we get to what it does.

ESG ratings are not a scorecard that sits on a website and gets ignored. They move money. When SustainAbility surveyed institutional investors in 2020, ESG ratings came out as the single most frequently referenced source of corporate ESG information — ahead of company reports, ahead of direct engagement. And CDP reports that in 2023, 85% of financial institutions disclosing through CDP used ESG ratings specifically to identify investment opportunities.

So put those together. These scores sit upstream of real capital allocation decisions, and the paper cites both of these in its very first footnote to make exactly that point.

Which means the question we are asking today is not academic hygiene. If something systematically inflates a rating, that distortion doesn't stay in a database — it ends up in somebody's pension fund.

01The questionThe puzzle

Ratings for the same company diverge —
and they diverge most where the rater has an owner in common.

The paper measures divergence directly: the average pairwise standard deviation of the ratings a firm receives from different agencies. Higher = the agencies agree less.

The standard account

Agencies define, scope and weight ESG differently, so they land on different numbers. Well documented, and conceptually innocent — nobody is doing anything wrong.

What this paper adds

Part of the divergence may come from who owns the rater. Ratings would then disagree for reasons that have nothing to do with the rated company at all.

Reading the chart

Disagreement measure follows Avramov et al. (2022). Wider literature: Gibson, Krueger & Schmidt (2021), Financial Analysts Journal 77.

Here is the puzzle this paper attaches itself to.

If you take one company and look up its ESG rating at several agencies, you very often get materially different answers. This is thoroughly documented — it is usually called the rater effect, or the ratings-disagreement literature. The standard explanation is technical and quite innocent: agencies define ESG differently, cover different indicators, weight them differently. Everyone acts in good faith and they land in different places.

What Tang, Yan and Yao propose is a different kind of explanation — that part of the divergence comes from who owns the rating agency.

And I want to show you their evidence for that now, even though it technically belongs to section three, because it defines the whole question. The paper measures disagreement directly: for each firm they take the ratings from different agencies, compute the average pairwise standard deviation, and that number is how much the agencies disagree about that company.

Then they ask: is disagreement larger for firms connected to the rater? It is. Among the four other agencies, the sister coefficient is +0.312 with a t-statistic of 4.42 in year t, and +0.276, t of 3.91, the following year. If you fold KLD back into the measure you get +0.183 and +0.207, t-statistics of 4.04 and 4.82. All four significant at the 1% level.

So the firms that share an owner with their rater are precisely the firms the market cannot agree about. That is the thread we pull for the next twenty minutes.

There is a reason this paper is being published now, and it is regulatory.

In April 2024, the European Union became the first jurisdiction in the world to formally regulate the transparency and integrity of ESG ratings. And the regulation is remarkably specific: it tasks ESMA, the European Securities and Markets Authority, with addressing conflicts of interest that may arise from the rating agency's own shareholders.

I want you to notice the sequence there, because it matters. The EU regulated a suspicion. It did not regulate in response to a body of published evidence showing that rater ownership actually distorts ratings, because that evidence largely did not exist yet.

This paper is, in effect, the test of the assumption the regulation rests on. Our fourth section comes back to whether the evidence justifies the rule.

Where does this sit in the literature?

Common ownership — the same institutional investors holding large stakes in multiple companies — is a genuinely crowded field. But almost all of it is horizontal: common ownership among competitors in the same industry, and what that does to competition, pricing and disclosure. Azar, Schmalz and Tecu in 2018, Park and co-authors in 2019, and others.

What is much thinner is cross-industry common ownership, where the same investor holds both a rating agency and the firms that agency rates. And that relationship is structurally different, because one of the two parties is grading the other.

The closest precedent comes from credit ratings. Kedia, Rajgopal and Zhou, in 2017, showed that Moody's assigns higher credit ratings to companies that share large shareholders with Moody's itself. That paper matters here for two reasons: it establishes the pattern in a rating market we understand well, and — importantly for section two — this paper borrows its definition of a “connected” firm directly from that study, rather than inventing a convenient new one.

01The questionTheoretical framework

Three theories. One identical prediction.

All three predict the same surface result, so finding a higher rating proves nothing on its own. Most of the paper exists to close two of these branches — we return to this diagram in section four.

The authors are deliberately agnostic ex ante: common ownership could plausibly improve rating accuracy or distort it.

This is the part I most want you to hold on to, because it is what makes the rest of the paper interesting.

On the left is the thing we observe: a sister firm gets a higher ESG rating. And there are three plausible reasons why — all three predicting exactly the same thing on the surface.

Branch one, information sharing. A shared owner sits on both sides and acts as a conduit. Either it helps the rating agency see ESG information about a complex company that it otherwise couldn't observe — or it works in the other direction, and helps the company understand the rater's methodology so it can disclose what actually gets scored. That draws on Hail, Kim and Zhang, 2021.

Branch two, conflict of interest. A large shareholder leans on the rating agency to be generous to its portfolio companies. Why would it bother? Because higher ESG ratings attract fund inflows and support higher management fees. That's Chen and Sun, 2024, and it mirrors the credit-rating conflict literature.

Branch three, investor activism. The shared owner genuinely engages with the company, the company genuinely improves, and the higher rating is simply correct. Dimson, Karakaş and Li, 2015.

Branch one is neutral-to-good. Branch three is unambiguously good. Branch two is the scandal. And they look identical if all you do is compare ratings.

Keep this picture in your head — my colleague in section four will put it back on screen with two of the three branches closed.

The agency at the centre of this study is KLD, and this diagram shows how one of its ratings is built.

KLD assesses firms across seven dimensions — environment, community, employee relations, diversity, product, humanity and governance. Within each, a firm scores a point for every strength criterion it meets and a point for every concern criterion it triggers. Those all collapse into a single number at the bottom: total strengths minus total concerns. So it's a net score, and it can be negative.

Why KLD? Because it is one of the earliest providers and has by far the longest usable series — from the S&P 500 in 1991, out to the Russell 3000 by 2003. It is also the agency most heavily used in academic ESG research, so the measure is familiar and comparable.

Two numbers to carry forward. Across the sample the mean rating is 0.13, and the standard deviation is 2.74. Please hold on to 2.74 — in section three that is the yardstick we use to judge whether a coefficient is economically meaningful or trivially small.

02Data & methodThe enabling fact

This study exists because of one acquisition.

Those new ties appeared for reasons unrelated to any rated firm's ESG quality — which is exactly why the same event is reused in section three as a quasi-natural experiment.

Tang, Yan & Yao (2025), Section 3. Ownership observable via Thomson Reuters 13-F institutional holdings.

Now, the single most important fact in the entire methodology, and it is a historical accident. Follow the timeline with me.

On the left: before 2010, KLD was private and mostly employee-owned. That means its shareholder base was simply invisible. You could not observe whether KLD shared an owner with any company it rated, so this research question could not be asked at all — not because nobody thought of it, but because the data did not exist.

The red marker: in 2010, MSCI acquired KLD. MSCI is a publicly listed index and data provider. Overnight, KLD's ownership became the ownership of a public company, and public company ownership is observable through 13-F filings.

And here is the elegant part, the third marker. MSCI already had large institutional shareholders, and those shareholders already held stakes in hundreds of companies that KLD rated. So the moment the deal closed, common-ownership ties appeared between the rater and those firms — for reasons that had absolutely nothing to do with any of those firms' ESG behaviour.

The shaded band is the sample window that opens at that moment and runs to 2015. And that same event gets used a second time, in section three, as a natural experiment.

02Data & methodSample

Three datasets, six years, one panel

The sample opens in 2010 because that is when the rater's ownership first becomes observable.

So what does the dataset actually look like?

The sample runs from 2010 to 2015. The start date is not a choice — it is the acquisition. Before 2010 there is no ownership data to work with.

They combine three sources. KLD supplies the ESG ratings, the dependent variable. Institutional ownership comes from Thomson Reuters 13-F filings — the quarterly holdings disclosures institutional investors must file with the SEC — and those are used twice: once to see who owns MSCI, and again to see what else those owners hold. Firm financials come from Compustat and CRSP, U.S. firms excluding financial services.

That gives 11,145 firm-year observations. And the bar at the top shows you the balance, which I think is important to be honest about. The red slice is the treated group: 832 sister firm-years, from 178 unique firms. Everything else — the grey — is 10,313 firm-years from 2,474 firms.

So sister firms are about seven and a half percent of the panel. That's a small treated group, and it is precisely why the authors run as many robustness checks as they do.

02Data & methodThe key variable

What makes a company a “sister firm”?

Why two conditions

Either one alone is harmless. Influence needs both ends — a stake in the rater, and a position worth protecting.

Why 0.25% is not arbitrary

It is the 75th percentile of portfolio weight: small as a percentage, top quarter as a position.

Why borrowed thresholds matter

Both cutoffs come from the Moody's study, so the definition could not be tuned until a result appeared.

Both thresholds are inherited from Kedia, Rajgopal & Zhou (2017) — not chosen to suit the result.

This is the key variable in the paper, so let me walk the diagram. Being a "sister firm" requires two things to be true at the same time.

At the top is the common owner — an institutional investor. Follow the left arrow: that investor has to be a large shareholder of the rater, which the authors define as owning at least 5% of MSCI's shares in a given year.

Now follow the right arrow: that same company has to actually matter to that investor. A rated company counts as a sister firm if it makes up at least 0.25% of that shareholder's investment portfolio. That sounds like a tiny number, and it's worth explaining why it isn't arbitrary: 0.25% is the 75th percentile of portfolio weight in the Thomson Reuters universe. So you are in the top quarter of that investor's holdings by weight.

And the horizontal arrow across the middle is the relationship that makes all this a problem: the agency on the left assigns the rating to the company on the right, while the same investor sits above both.

The important methodological point is that neither threshold was invented here. Both come from the Kedia, Rajgopal and Zhou Moody's study. That matters because it means the authors did not tune their definition until a result appeared — they inherited it from the closest existing paper. And they don't leave it there: they re-run everything at a 4% ownership cutoff and push the portfolio-weight threshold up to 5%.

The baseline is a firm-level panel regression, and it is deliberately unglamorous. ESG rating for firm i in year t, regressed on the Sister dummy, plus a vector of firm characteristics. The coefficient alpha-one, on Sister, is the whole object of interest. In the fullest specification they add industry-by-year fixed effects and cluster standard errors at the firm level.

The control set is worth a moment, because the obvious objection to this paper is: "big institutional investors hold big companies, and big companies have better ESG ratings — you've just rediscovered firm size." So they control for total assets, firm age, sales per employee, institutional ownership and analyst coverage; and then the whole financial profile — book-to-market, leverage, ROA, prior returns, capex, R&D, dividends, cash, advertising. You'll see in a moment that size alone absorbs a large chunk of the raw effect.

My favourite control is the third column: a blue-state dummy, for firms headquartered in Democratic-leaning states. That's a proxy for the local political climate around ESG — the idea being that firms in progressive states might both attract certain investors and score better on ESG for reasons unrelated to any ownership tie.

And industry-by-year fixed effects mean we are ultimately comparing firms within the same industry in the same year.

02Data & methodIdentification

The obvious objection: reverse causality

Three designs with three different failure modes. No single one is conclusive on its own; the point is that a spurious result would have to survive all three.

Over to Presenter 3 — what all of this actually produced.

Now the hard part. There is an obvious objection to everything I've described, and the authors know it.

The objection is reverse causality. Maybe genuinely good-ESG firms attract exactly the kind of large institutional investors that also hold MSCI. In that world, ownership doesn't cause the rating — unobserved firm quality causes both. No amount of control variables fully settles that.

So they attack it three ways.

First, a quasi-natural experiment. They use the 2010 acquisition as a difference-in-differences design. The logic: the connection appeared because of a corporate transaction between MSCI and KLD, not because of anything the rated firms did. So if ratings rise only after the tie appears, and not before, the reverse-causality story becomes very hard to tell.

Second, cross-rater falsification. They pull the same firms' ESG ratings from four independent agencies — Bloomberg, Refinitiv, Sustainalytics and TruValue Labs — and rescale them to KLD's units. This is a genuine falsification test. If sister firms really are better ESG performers, everybody should score them higher. If only KLD does, then the effect lives in the relationship, not in the company.

Third, a robustness battery: different ownership cutoffs, different sister thresholds tested as a dose-response, a continuous rather than binary connection measure, propensity-score matching, and an Oster bounding test for omitted-variable bias.

So — three designs, three different failure modes. Over to my colleague for what they found.

Let's start with the headline, and I want to show it as a specification table because the shrinkage is as informative as the final number.

Column one: just the Sister dummy, no controls at all. The coefficient is 2.775, t-statistic 10.38, adjusted R-squared 0.070. So raw sister firms are rated about 2.775 notches higher. That's a big gap — recall the standard deviation of the whole rating distribution is only 2.74.

But that number is naive, and the authors know it. Column two adds one single control: total assets. The coefficient collapses from 2.775 to 1.199, t-statistic 4.97, and the R-squared jumps to 0.237. So more than half the raw gap was just firm size. Big investors hold big companies; big companies score better.

Column three is the real test. Full controls, the blue-state dummy, and industry-by-year fixed effects. The coefficient falls again, to 0.795, t-statistic 3.78, adjusted R-squared 0.390. Still significant at the 1% level.

Is 0.795 big? That is roughly 29% of one standard deviation. So: comparing two firms in the same industry in the same year, with the same size, profitability, leverage, R&D, analyst coverage and political geography, the one that shares a large shareholder with the rater scores about a third of a standard deviation higher.

And one more thing that argues against noise: the effect gets stronger as you require the firm to matter more to that shareholder. We will put that gradient on screen in a few slides, when we test the mechanisms.

Here is the objection I would have raised if I were the referee. Maybe KLD isn't biased — maybe KLD is just better. Maybe the shared owner genuinely gives KLD better information, KLD sees real quality earlier, and everyone else is simply behind.

This is the test that kills that story, and I think it's the most elegant thing in the paper.

They take the same firms and pull ESG ratings from four independent agencies — Bloomberg, Refinitiv, Sustainalytics and TruValue Labs — and rescale everything to a common metric.

The top bar is the relative rating: KLD minus the average of the other four. The Sister coefficient there is plus 2.662, significant at the 1% level. So yes, KLD rates its sister firms well above what everyone else does.

Now the three bars below, which are the average rating given by those other four raters. In year t: minus 0.554, not significant. So the other raters see nothing special about these companies at all.

And here's the part that matters — follow the dotted line. If KLD were merely early, if it were genuinely spotting real ESG improvement before the others, then the others should catch up over time. Branch one, information sharing. This gets partial support, but not the flattering half.

Here's the test that fails. If a shared owner is genuinely helping the rater understand a company, that help should be worth most where the company is hardest to analyse. So the effect should be bigger for complex firms. It isn't. Firm complexity doesn't moderate the effect at all.

Here's the test that passes, and it's revealing. The authors split KLD's indicators into financially material and immaterial components, using SASB's sector-specific materiality standards. Branch three, investor activism. This one is simply not supported. If the owner were genuinely engaging and driving real improvement, the effect should concentrate among owners who actually engage. It doesn't. Filing SRI shareholder proposals: no difference. Being a UN PRI signatory: no difference. Being classified as an ESG-focused investor: no difference.

The only thing that predicts the rating bump is the size of the stake. And stake size is a measure of influence, not of engagement.

So that leaves branch two — conflict of interest — and this is the one the evidence keeps fitting. Three separate pieces.

First, dose-response — that is the chart on the left, redrawn from Figure 3 of the paper. The horizontal axis is how large a slice of the shareholder's portfolio a company has to be before it counts as a sister firm. As you walk right, you are looking at firms that matter more and more to that owner. And the coefficient climbs: from about one rating notch at the 1% cutoff up to 1.773 at the 5% cutoff — more than double the 0.795 baseline. That 1.773 is the one value the paper prints, which is why it is the only point I have labelled.

Two honest notes on this chart. The dashed bars are the published error bars, and they are wide — at the 1% cutoff the interval nearly touches zero. And the paper's text describes the series as increasing monotonically, but on its own figure the fifth point sits a hair below the fourth. The gradient is clearly there; "monotonic" is slightly generous.

Why does the gradient matter? Because it is what influence predicts and the other two branches do not. The premium also rises with the owner's stake in MSCI and with its assets under management — the more leverage over the rater, the more inflation.

Second, trading behaviour, and this one I find quite damning. Large blockholders tend to reduce their holdings in a sister firm in the year or two after that firm's rating goes up. Think about what that means. If you were genuinely engaging with a company to improve it, you'd stay. Selling down after the rating rises looks much more like realising a benefit you helped create.

Third, reversal. Firms that stop being sister firms — where the ownership tie is severed — see their ratings subsequently marked back down. So the premium travels with the relationship, not with the company. The company didn't get worse; it just stopped being connected.

I want to be fair here: none of these three is a smoking gun on its own. Each has an alternative reading if you work at it. The argument is cumulative — three independent behaviours, all pointing the same direction.

This is the finding I'd put on the exam, because it moves the paper from "interesting" to "consequential".

If a higher rating meant anything real, sister firms should go on to behave better. The authors test that directly, using Violation Tracker — regulatory and CSR violation data from Good Jobs First — and RepRisk for negative ESG news.

They find the opposite, and the bars show it. Being a sister firm is associated with a higher probability of at least one regulatory violation in the following year. Any violation: 8.8 percentage points higher, t-statistic 3.78. Labor violations, almost as large: 8.7 points. Environmental: 4.7 points. Consumer: 4.3 points. All statistically significant — environmental at the 5% level, the other three at 1%.

One clarification so I don't mislead you: these are increases in probability, in percentage points — not the level of risk. The claim is "meaningfully more likely to offend", not "an 8.8% chance".

Same story with RepRisk: sister firms accumulate significantly more negative ESG incidents afterwards, even holding their prior level of ESG concerns constant.

And the concentration is the clincher. The effect is strongest among the sister firms whose ratings actually rose. So the firms that got the biggest boost went on to have the worst record.

That is the opposite of what a rating is supposed to do. It isn't just uninformative — it points the wrong way. Over to my colleague for what we do with that.

Two branches close. One survives.

Branch one, information sharing, is struck through but only partially: complexity doesn't moderate the effect, and the gains land on the immaterial indicators. Everything we have shown you lives on one side of a line, and I want to make that line explicit.

So, three audiences.

For investors: you buy the number and you inherit the structure. A rating from a connected rater may be describing a company that is quietly riskier than the score suggests, because those are precisely the firms that go on to collect more violations and more negative news.

For corporate managers: the boost lands on the financially immaterial indicators. That is the signature of optimising for what gets measured rather than what matters. It scores well. It does not appear to make the company better.

For regulators: this is close to a ready-made justification for what the EU did in April 2024. The bias is real, it is worth roughly 29% of a standard deviation, and — critically — it is detectable with public ownership data. Credit rating agencies went through exactly this reckoning after 2008, about who paid them and who owned them. ESG ratings are arriving at the same place about fifteen years later.

What the paper cannot tell us

1

A single rating agency

Everything rests on KLD — not because it is representative, but because it is the only agency whose ownership was ever observable. We cannot say whether other raters behave the same way.

2

Tang, Yan & Yao (2025), concluding section. Oster (2019) bounding test.

Before we finish, the limitations — and I want to give these proper time, because the authors are unusually honest about them and that honesty is part of why the paper is credible.

First, it rests on a single rating agency. Everything you have heard is about KLD. And KLD was not chosen because it is representative — it was chosen because it is the only agency whose ownership was ever observable, thanks to the MSCI acquisition. So we genuinely cannot say whether Sustainalytics or Refinitiv behave the same way. It is entirely possible KLD is unusual.

Second, the window is early and short. 2010 to 2015 is a young, still-forming ESG ratings market. The industry today is much larger, more consolidated and more scrutinised. The authors explicitly caution readers about external validity to the present day.

Third, several sub-tests return nulls. Firm complexity as a moderator — null. The activism proxies — null. And to their credit the authors write, in plain language, that they "do not claim an indisputable conclusion on which channel is ultimately at work". They are saying: our evidence is more consistent with owner influence than the alternatives. That is a weaker and more honest claim than "we proved it".

Fourth, residual omitted-variable risk, as in any observational corporate finance study. The Oster bounding test says unobservable factors would need to be roughly 2.8 times more important than all the included controls combined to overturn the result. That's reassuring. It is not the same as dispositive.

So: a strong paper, appropriately hedged.

AAppendixVariables and terms

Every threshold and term, defined

Sister firm
A rated company that accounts for at least 0.25% of the portfolio of an institution which itself owns at least 5% of the rating agency. Both conditions must hold in the same year.
Large shareholder of the rater
An institution owning ≥ 5% of MSCI’s shares in a given year, from 13-F filings. Robustness re-runs the whole analysis at a 4% cutoff.
ESG rating (KLD)
Total strengths minus total concerns across seven dimensions. One net figure per firm-year, and it can be negative. Sample mean 0.13, standard deviation 2.74.
Relative ESG rating
KLD’s rating minus the average of Bloomberg, Refinitiv, Sustainalytics and TruValue Labs for the same firm-year, rescaled to a common metric.
Rating disagreement
Average pairwise standard deviation of the ratings a firm receives across agencies, scaled by total assets. Higher means the agencies agree less. Measure follows Avramov et al. (2022).
Material / immaterial
KLD’s indicators mapped against SASB sector standards: material means likely to affect financial condition or operating performance. Mapping follows Khan, Serafeim & Yoon (2016).
Treat and Post
Treat = firms that became sister firms because MSCI acquired KLD in 2010. Post = the years after the acquisition. Their interaction is the causal estimate.
Pseudo sister
A firm held by the same shareholders before those shareholders owned the rater. The pre-period placebo on slide 20.
Departed sister
A former sister firm whose position the rater’s large shareholder has since cut below the threshold, severing the tie.
Oster (2019) bound
How much more important unobserved factors would have to be than every included control in order to overturn the coefficient. Here, 2.8×.
Blue-state dummy
Equals one if the firm is headquartered in a Democratic-leaning state — a control for the local political climate around ESG.
RepRisk & Violation Tracker
RepRisk covers ESG incidents and business-conduct risk since 2007. Violation Tracker (Good Jobs First) records regulatory and CSR violations. Both are used to test what happens after the rating.
Definitions as given in Tang, Yan & Yao (2025), sections 3.1–3.3 and the notes to Tables 1, 2, 3, 5 and 9.

Reference slide. If a question turns on what exactly counts as a sister firm, or what "immaterial" means here, this is the slide to go to.

The two numbers worth having in your head: 5% of the rater, and 0.25% of the owner's portfolio. Both are inherited from Kedia, Rajgopal and Zhou's credit-rating study, so neither was chosen to suit this result.
